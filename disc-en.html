What Is the DISC Behavioral Style Test?

The DISC behavioral style test is based on a theory put forward by psychologist William Marston in his 1928 book Emotions of Normal People. It sorts human behavior into four tendencies: Dominance (D), Influence (I), Steadiness (S), and Conscientiousness (C).

To make it easier for the general public to remember and understand, later practitioners began describing DISC styles with animals. The common ones are the D Tiger type, I Peacock type, S Koala type, and C Owl type. The animals add a visual and personality association that quickly builds a vivid picture.

Three more things to keep in mind:

  • This is an assessment tool. It measures emotional and behavioral tendencies, not how smart you are or how capable you are.
  • Everyone has all four behavioral styles, just in different proportions. No type is better than another, and the proportions can also show up differently depending on the situation.
  • The test is a reference tool for getting to know yourself and understanding others. It is not for labeling people or boxing yourself in.

The DISC theory tells the four styles apart along two main axes:

  1. X axis: Where your attention usually goes when you handle things, on the "task" or on the "people."
  2. Y axis: Whether you tend to "take the initiative" or to "adapt and go along" when facing a situation.

The History of the DISC Behavioral Style Test

The DISC test began with the American psychologist William Moulton Marston. In 1928 he published Emotions of Normal People, which explored the emotions and behavior of "normal people" and proposed that along the two dimensions above, people show four different tendencies.

However, Marston himself never built a test or turned the idea into a fixed product. It was later that others turned these ideas into a questionnaire people could answer:

  • Around the 1950s: Industrial psychologist Walter Clarke created the first self-report questionnaire based on this theory, originally used to select salespeople.
  • 1970s: John Geier and others developed a widely used version, and after that various organizations released their own DISC tools.

That is why there are many DISC tests on the market, each with different questions, reports, and wording, and their results cannot be compared directly. As for the animal comparisons, they are a popular shorthand added later to make the styles easier to remember, and they are not part of the original theory.

What Can the DISC Behavioral Style Test Be Used For?

The most useful thing about DISC is that it gives everyone a shared language, so people can talk about "we do things differently" instead of blaming each other. Start with this overview table, because the three points below all draw on it:

I PeacockLifting the mood, coming up with lots of ideasWorking alone for long stretches with no responseStart with feelings and give them room to express themselves
S KoalaSteady support, sustaining things over the long runBeing rushed to decide, or sudden changesGive them time to digest it and do not push for an answer on the spot
C OwlTaking care of details, guarding qualityNot enough information, unclear standardsCome prepared with data and reasons

1. Getting to know yourself

Look at the "Shines when" column to learn your strengths, so you can put your energy there with confidence. Then look at the "Finds it hard when" column to learn which situations call for looking after yourself early. Think back to a recent time when things went especially smoothly or felt especially draining, and compare it with the table. It usually clicks.

2. Improving communication

Look at the "When talking with them" column. Saying the same thing in a different way makes it easier for the other person to take in. This is not about pleasing people. It is about having fewer moments of "I clearly explained it, so why didn't they get it?"

3. Teamwork and career exploration

Once you see the differences, an "attitude problem" can become a division of labor: turn to D for decisions and sprints, I for explaining things to outsiders, S for long-term care, and C for checking the details. It will not tell you which career to choose, but it can help you work out the work pace and environment you prefer.

One thing to remember when using it: use it to ask questions, not to draw conclusions.

What Are the Limitations of the DISC Behavioral Style Test?

1. Different versions

Marston never built a test himself. The versions on the market are commercial products developed independently, with different questions and scoring, so it is perfectly normal for the same person to get different results on different platforms. It is generally considered helpful for self-understanding, but it cannot predict whose job performance will be better.

2. Results can vary

Your result is affected by your mood when you answer, which occasion or situation you have in mind, and how you want to be seen. The four animals are there to be memorable, but real people are continuous and many-sided, and it is common for two styles to both be strong. Someone might even test as an "S Koala type" themselves while others see them as a "D Tiger type," two completely different results. That gap can help you learn about the "blind self" described in the Johari Window theory. (P.S. You can also try the "How others see you" feature in the test.)

3. Not suited to these uses

Do not use it to decide hiring or promotions. It is not a psychological or health diagnosis, and it cannot tell you whether a relationship will work. If you are struggling emotionally, please see a professional counseling psychologist or doctor. DISC results are only a reference. It is a mirror that helps you see one side of yourself, not a verdict.

FAQ

Is a person only one animal?

No. Everyone has all four tendencies, and it is common for two of them to both be strong.

Is the DISC behavioral style test accurate?

The DISC test describes your behavioral tendencies based on your own answers. It is good for getting to know yourself and improving communication. It is not a skills score or a diagnostic tool, and the results are for reference only.

Will my result stay the same for life?

Not necessarily. Behavior adjusts with the situation, your role, and your stage of life. The test reflects your tendencies at the time you answered.
